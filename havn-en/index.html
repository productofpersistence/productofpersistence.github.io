How your spending splits
A short guide: splitting your budget, sorting your spending, keeping debt in check

The 50/30/20 rule. A simple way to balance a budget, made popular by Elizabeth Warren (a Harvard professor, later a US senator) in her book All Your Worth (2005). The idea: split your after-tax income into a few clear buckets, so you never have to police every dollar.

50% Needs. What you have to pay to live and work: rent or mortgage, groceries, power, water, transport, insurance, and the payments on something you genuinely need (your home, the car you drive to work). If your needs run over 50%, that is extremely common (high rent, a mortgage, taxes): it is not a personal failure. Adjust your target rule and aim to raise your income instead.

30% Wants. What makes life good but you could live without, paid in cash: eating out, going out, streaming, shopping for fun, hobbies, vacations.

20% Future = Savings + Debt. The rule puts together the two things that build what you own: what you save and what you pay down on consumer debt. That is why Debt sits right next to Savings and nowhere else. With no debt, the whole 20% goes to savings.

Note: your savings are not really an expense, they are paying yourself first. They are listed with everything else so that every dollar has a job, but that money stays yours.

So where does consumer debt fit? Debt taken on for something non-essential (a TV, furniture) is paid down first, out of that bucket, before you save: it costs you more in interest than your savings earn. Clear it to zero, then move the whole share to savings.

“Is a loan a Need or a Debt?” It depends on what you took it out for. For something you need to live or work (a home, a car), the payment is a Need, like rent. For something non-essential (a TV), it is a Debt. Keeping it separate shows you how much of your paycheck is still paying for things you already bought.

How much to save, and how much debt at most? The rule aims at 20% for your future (saving and paying down debt together). It sets no ceiling on debt: that is not its job. A common second yardstick is to keep consumer-debt payments under about 10 to 15% of your income. Lenders, for their part, usually turn down new credit once all your debt payments, mortgage included, go past about 36% of your gross income: that is the debt-to-income ratio. Past those marks, debt weighs too much and becomes priority number one.

The three-second sorting test: "If I do not pay this one this month, does it break my life, my housing, my ability to eat or to work?" If yes, it is a Need. If it is a choice, it is a Want.

50/30/20 is a yardstick, not a law, and it is not the only one. You can change your target rule just above the chart (or set your own percentages). What matters is a balance you can hold for years, not a perfect number.
